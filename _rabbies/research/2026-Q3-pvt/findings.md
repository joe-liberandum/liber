# Findings: Private Tour Bookers

**Study:** Rabbie's private tour booker interviews
**Fieldwork:** 2 to 16 September 2026
**Participants:** 6, all converted customers (JT, AM, MS, HC, CP, KA)
**Method:** Moderated depth interviews, 28 to 57 minutes, remote

Counts below are out of 6 unless a finding applies only to a subgroup, in which case the subgroup is stated.

## Headline

Private tour customers are not buying a better tour. They are buying someone to take responsibility for a group.

Five of six bookers were the appointed organiser for a party they did not fully control. They are not asking for luxury, they are asking to be told what to do by someone who knows the country. Rabbie's wins this business on the first reply and loses ground on it. The three fixes that matter most are a planning call, structured capture of group needs, and accommodation named early enough to be refused. None of them are website features.

The website is barely in this decision. The sale happens in the first email, and the failures happen in accommodation and in the email thread.

The proposal is the product demo. Where it led, Rabbie's beat a named competitor in the same week. Where it did not, the customer booked anyway and felt the gap.

"I'm the guy that is gonna take the flak from the other 15 people."

## Findings

### F1 The private tour is bought to manage a group, not to buy a premium product

**Evidence.** 5 of 6 (JT, AM, HC, CP, KA) were the appointed organiser for a party they did not fully control, and all five described the purchase as removing variables rather than adding quality. Party sizes were 8, 11, 10, 16 and 4. The constraints named as reasons for going private were group composition, not taste: motion sickness across most of a party and an age range of 11 to 70 (JT), a walker user aged 78 (AM), grandparents in their 80s who will not climb stairs and a coeliac cousin (HC), 16 people aged roughly 60 plus who still want to walk (CP), and a refusal to drive on the left (KA). CP stated the emotional job directly: he is the one who takes the flak from the other 15 people.

**Dissent.** MS does not fit. Her party was two, there was no group to manage, and her stated reasons were privacy, prior bad experiences with strangers on group tours, and wanting her husband's first trip to Europe to go well. She bought control, not delegation. HC fits partially and complicates the picture in a different way: she did the organising but her grandparents were the payers, so the buyer and the organiser were different people, which no other case had.

**Confidence.** High for the group case, on 5 of 6 with consistent and specific reasoning. Low for anything said about the couple who buy private, which rests on one interview.

---

### F2 Where a live competitor exists, the first reply decides the sale

**Evidence.** 2 of 6 ran a genuine parallel comparison and both switched to Rabbie's on the strength of the proposal rather than anything else. JT contacted Rabbie's and Discover Scotland Tours with the same brief; Rabbie's gave an indicative day rate unprompted, explained why Blair Castle suited her group better than Stirling Castle, and offered to buy the attraction tickets. She described the competitor as not being as upfront with information. MS contacted Rabbie's and one other Scottish operator; the other returned a stock group itinerary relabelled as private, which she said did not feel like something she would pay extra for.

**Dissent.** 4 of 6 never ran a comparison at all, so the mechanism was never tested on them. AM went straight to Rabbie's on a four year old day tour memory and said she did not really look at other operators. HC went straight there on three prior trips. CP went straight there on a recommendation from another operator plus two prior Rabbie's tours. KA did a couple of quick checks on other names from a Facebook group and explicitly did not price shop. For these four the decision was closed before the first reply arrived.

**Confidence.** Medium. Only two observations of the mechanism, but they are independent, consistent, and both name the same failure in the competitor. The four non-comparers do not contradict the finding; they show it only applies to a minority of enquiries, and we do not know how large that minority is in the real enquiry pool.

---

### F3 Low knowledge buyers cannot answer an open brief, and want to be told

**Evidence.** 4 of 6 wanted the operator to lead. CP was the most explicit: asked what he wanted to do, his answer was that he did not know and Rabbie's should tell him. KA, asked what he would change for a second trip, said he would hand the itinerary over entirely and ask for the hidden gems the locals know. JT described the value of being offered an A or B choice rather than an open question, saying it made her feel empowered without being paralysed. HC wanted more suggestions for things to do and ended up sourcing the best day of her trip herself.

**Dissent.** AM is a clear counter. She arrived with a firm view, used a published Rabbie's group tour as the skeleton, and said she did not depend on them for the itinerary so much as for making the arrangements. KA is a partial counter in the other direction: he arrived with a merged itinerary assembled from a failed travel agent's proposals, so what he valued was correction, not creation. He praised being told he was doing too much. His stated preference for a fully handed over itinerary applies to a second trip, after he had exhausted the obvious.

**Confidence.** High that the open brief fails for people with no fixed wish list. The dissent is the useful part: two of the four wanted correction rather than creation, and AM wanted neither. The operational implication is detection, not a single script.

---

### F4 Email becomes the system of record for the booking and fails at the top end of complexity

**Evidence.** 2 of 6 reported concrete failures. AM finished with a chain of 80 to 90 linked emails and had to search it to recover her own reference number, describing it as unwieldy. Her accessibility requirement was lost inside that chain and resulted in a service failure on tour. HC had Rabbie's replies land repeatedly in her junk folder, including inside an existing thread she had replied to, causing her to chase replies that had already been sent a week earlier.

**Dissent.** 3 of 6 are actively content with email. HC, despite the junk issue, prefers long informative emails and PDFs and said she likes everything in one message so there is less back and forth; she did not use any manage booking function. KA was content with email and PDFs and advised Rabbie's not to turn itself into an IT company, recommending a third party tool instead. MS said email is usually the best way for her. AM is the only participant asking for a login.

**Confidence.** Medium. The failure is real and consequential but concentrated in the longest, most complex, most needs heavy booking in the sample. This does not support an account system on current evidence. It supports fixing the specific failures.

---

### F5 Voice contact is wanted at the start of complex trips, not throughout

**Evidence.** 4 of 6 wanted a call and all four located it at the planning stage rather than across the booking. CP prefers phone and person to person contact over email and said more in depth conversation was his only major piece of feedback. KA said that at this trip value he expects to get on the phone. AM wanted video to put a face to the person and proposed extended or shifted working hours, then proposed the mechanism herself after using a booked slot to attend this interview. HC had exactly one call, after the first large email, and said that was good for talking ideas through so the consultant had a better idea what to propose.

**Dissent.** 2 of 6 did not want it. MS never perceived channel or response time as a barrier and rated the booking experience 9 out of 10 for an unrelated reason. JT never raised it, completed everything through form and email, and rated the experience 10 out of 10. Both are single day bookings.

**Confidence.** High for multi day bookings. The two dissenters are the two day tour bookers, which sharpens the finding rather than weakening it: demand for voice contact tracks trip length and the absence of a fixed plan, not customer preference in general. HC's single call also suggests the requirement is one conversation, not an ongoing channel.

---

### F6 Accommodation is the weakest operational link in the multi day private product

**Evidence.** 4 of 4 multi day bookers had an accommodation problem, and they were four different problems. AM, who uses a walker, was placed in a hotel with 47 steps and no lift, with accessible rooms available but not booked; she resolved it herself at the property. CP and his wife read reviews on every proposed property, found several with poor recent reviews, and went back to Rabbie's to refuse them. KA booked all his own hotels and was told by his driver that this had been the right call because Rabbie's sometimes books too late to secure the properties customers want. HC ended up in the second choice hotel in Tobermory because the first had no availability.

**Dissent.** JT and MS had no accommodation in scope, both being single day tours, so 2 of 6 cannot speak to it. Within the multi day group there is no dissent, which is itself worth noting and is unusual in this study.

**Confidence.** High within multi day private tours, on 4 of 4 with distinct and independently described failures. Not applicable to day tours. The absence of any counter case in the subgroup is a signal, though 4 is a small subgroup.

---

### F7 Price rarely blocks the booking, but unexplained price creates doubt that persists

**Evidence.** 5 of 6 did not treat price as a barrier. AM set an internal ceiling of around $4,000 per person and Rabbie's came in under it, with no negotiation. CP said price always shocks everybody and proceeds regardless. KA did not price shop at all and exceeded his own $35,000 budget. JT asked Rabbie's to match a cheaper competitor quote and they did, and she judged private worth roughly 1.5 to 2 times the group tour price. HC said affordability was never the goal; a seamless trip was.

**Dissent.** MS did pause, and hers is the most useful data point in the finding because her concern was legibility rather than amount. Months after the trip she still could not account for part of the cost, noting that at the end of the day it was a guide with a vehicle, with no entries or lunch included. KA is a partial counter too: comfortable with the price, but he had to reverse engineer the driver's earnings from a spreadsheet because the bundle was opaque.

**Confidence.** Medium. The direction is consistent, but this is the finding most damaged by the sample. Every participant converted, so price driven abandonment is structurally invisible here. Treat the finding as "price did not block these six", not as "price does not block".

---

### F8 The guide is the product, and it does no work before the booking

**Evidence.** 6 of 6 volunteered the driver guide as the high point of the trip, unprompted, and 4 named them. The praise is specific rather than general: reading the group and adjusting the day (JT), going above and beyond on access needs (AM), hustling to fit in an unplanned stop (MS), physically supporting two elderly travellers on Staffa and rebooking an entire day's activities when someone fell ill (HC), fitting the group and being kind as well as knowledgeable (CP), and delivering history in a way that held attention for days (KA). No participant cited anything the website said about guides as a reason for choosing Rabbie's.

**Dissent.** None on the guide being the high point, which should make us suspicious, and there are two qualifications worth keeping. KA's wife got bored of the history delivery over time and he noted the van had no microphone, so the content was both too much and hard to hear from the back row. CP wished for more time off the bus and noted his driver was explicit that leading walks was not his job.

**Confidence.** High that the guide drives satisfaction and advocacy after the trip. Lower on the second half of the statement, which rests on an absence rather than an observation. We did not show anyone guide content and measure a response, so we know it did not come up, not that it would not work.

---

### F9 Rabbie's is filed mentally as a Scotland company

**Evidence.** 2 of 6 stated the assumption directly and unprompted. AM said she had assumed Rabbie's was mostly a Scottish tour company, and had wanted a private trip in Europe for the week preceding Scotland, did not find anything affordable and private, and gave that week to another operator. MS said she only presumed they did Scotland and called it her own mistake.

**Dissent.** CP was asked the same question and was aware, saying he had been on the website for ten years. HC and KA were not asked directly and did not raise it, so their position is unknown rather than supportive.

**Confidence.** Medium. Only 2 of 6 with a clear counter case, and a third of the sample was never asked. It is raised to medium rather than low because it corroborates a pattern already established in the earlier booker research across a larger sample.

---

### F10 Institutional scale is bought deliberately, as insurance against cancellation

**Evidence.** 3 of 6 chose partly on size and permanence. KA was the clearest: he had a fear of smaller operators being too small, had read repeatedly in a Facebook group about customers having guides cancelled on them, and said he never felt that would happen with Rabbie's. He and his party met travellers mid trip whose tour company had bailed on them. JT chose Rabbie's over a local operator because with a larger organisation you know what to expect. CP values the range that lets an economically mixed group travel together, from hostel to castle.

**Dissent.** CP undercuts the size argument even as he supports the reliability one. His route to Rabbie's was a personal recommendation from a sole proprietor in Ireland, Wolfhound Tours, whom he has used repeatedly and rates extremely highly, and he has repeatedly booked small operators elsewhere. Size is a proxy here. The thing being bought is the confidence that the trip will run.

**Confidence.** Medium. 3 of 6, with one participant simultaneously supporting and complicating it. The underlying want is reliability, and scale is only one way of signalling it.

---

### F11 The website is close to absent from the private tour decision, with one exception that matters

**Evidence.** 4 of 6 spent under ten minutes on site before making contact. KA, asked whether he looked around, said no, because it was a private tour and he wanted Rabbie's to figure it out. MS gave it five to ten minutes. HC found the private tours tab, read it, glanced at some group tours and enquired. CP went straight to it. When HC and JT were walked through the current private tours page live, neither identified a question it failed to answer.

**Dissent.** JT is the exception and the most useful behaviour observed in the study. She used the scheduled tour pages as a learning tool and a price anchor before enquiring at all: filtering by destination and duration, discovering the Kelpies and Aberfoyle for the first time, then running a group tour through the passenger selector purely to see what a party of her size would cost, without ever reaching checkout. CP is a softer counter, in that "went straight to it" understates ten years of prior exposure to the site.

**Confidence.** High on the behaviour, which is consistent and directly observed. Medium on what it means. All six were substantially convinced before arriving, so the site may be absent because it is not needed, or absent because it is not good enough to detain them. This study cannot separate those.

---

### F12 Post tour feedback collection is inconsistent

**Evidence.** The sample splits. 2 of 6 confirmed they received no survey: AM, who had a service failure worth capturing, and MS, who actively wanted one in order to praise her guide and was disappointed not to get it. 2 of 6 received one: HC, who did not complete it, and KA, who did. 2 of 6 are unknown: CP assumed his party had received them but did not confirm, and JT was not asked.

**Dissent.** Not applicable in the usual sense. The split is the finding. Nobody described a consistent process.

**Confidence.** High that delivery is inconsistent, because a 2 and 2 split across four confirmed cases cannot be explained by a working process. Low on the cause, which was not investigated.

---

### F13 Group needs are collected in prose, inside email, and that is where they fail

**Evidence.** 4 of 6 had material group needs to communicate and all four handled it informally. AM's walking limitation was communicated in the email chain and not acted on when accommodation was booked; she contrasted this directly with another operator's platform that required a per person entry for physical and dietary limitations, noting that although tedious it kept everything in one place. HC screened destinations herself against a coeliac cousin and grandparents who will not climb stairs, and ruled out the Outer Hebrides and Skye on those grounds before Rabbie's was involved in the choice. JT supplied ages, interests and a no boat tours constraint in prose by email. CP set expectations with his own group rather than relying on the operator.

**Dissent.** MS had no complex needs to communicate, so 1 of 6 had no exposure to the problem. No participant defended the current approach, but equally only one experienced a failure from it, and the other three worked around it successfully.

**Confidence.** High that the information exists in every booking and is unstructured. Medium on impact, since one failure in four exposed bookings is a small base, though the single failure was severe.

## What this does not tell us

- **Why anyone enquired and did not book.** Every participant converted. This is the same structural gap already recorded on the programme: booker only recruitment cannot reach non converting enquirers. Nothing here should be read as explaining the enquiry to booking drop off.
- **Whether price causes abandonment.** Related to the above and worth separating. F7 says price did not block these six. It cannot say price does not block. Any use of F7 to argue against price transparency work is a misuse of it.
- **Whether a discovery call converts.** Four participants said they wanted one. None of them was lost for the lack of it. We have stated preference, not evidence of commercial effect. The call recommendation rests on service quality and error reduction, not on a measured conversion gain.
- **Whether an account or itinerary hub would be used.** One participant wants it, three are content with email and PDFs, one advised against building it. This study cannot size the demand.
- **The two person private tour buyer.** One participant, MS. Her motivations, price sensitivity and information needs differ from the group organisers on almost every dimension. Treat anything drawn from her as a hypothesis.
- **Non North American markets.** Five of six are North American and the sixth is a UK resident hosting Americans. Nothing here speaks to Australia, New Zealand or continental Europe, all of which have different booking windows and different time zone realities for the call recommendation.
- **The mobile private tours experience.** HC researched primarily on her phone but could not share her screen, so the mobile page was described to her rather than observed. No mobile observation exists in this round.
- **How large the comparison shopping segment is.** F2 applies to two of six. Whether live competitive comparison is a minority or majority behaviour among real private tour enquirers is unknown and would need enquiry level data, not interviews.
- **Whether guide content pre booking would change anything.** F8 records an absence. No participant was shown guide content and asked to respond to it.
- **The cost and margin of better curation.** CP raised this himself, asking who pays for the driver's hour. Every recommendation that adds consultant or guide time to the pre sale phase has an unmeasured cost against an unmeasured conversion benefit.
- **Seasonality and booking window effects.** Booking dates in this sample range from one month out (MS) to twelve months out (AM). With six participants this cannot be separated from trip type or party size.

## Implications

*Interpretation, not evidence. Each line names the findings it rests on so the two stay separable.*

**The highest leverage point in the private tour journey is the first reply, not the website.** F2, F3 and F11 together suggest the proposal is doing the work the site is usually credited with. That reframes where effort should go: a better opening reply is worth more than a better private tours page for this segment. The counterweight is F11's dissent, JT, who shows the site still has a real job for people who do not yet know the country or the price.

**Detection should come before scripting.** F3's dissent is the operational point. CP wanted creation, KA wanted correction, AM wanted neither. A single improved opening script would serve one of the three and irritate the other two. The consultant needs a fast way to work out which of the three is in front of them, which is a qualification question, not a template.

**The call recommendation should be scoped, not universal.** F5 shows demand concentrated in multi day bookings with no fixed plan, and HC suggests one conversation is enough. Offering a bookable slot on qualifying enquiries is a smaller and more defensible commitment than extending hours across the board, and it is the version AM herself proposed.

**Accommodation is a process fix and probably the highest value one.** F6 is the only finding with no dissent in its applicable subgroup, and F1 explains why it hurts more here than elsewhere: the organiser absorbs the blame personally. Naming properties earlier with a veto window converts a trust risk into a shared decision. This does not need the website.

**Structured needs capture is cheap and prevents the worst failure observed.** F13 and F6 intersect at AM. The information already exists in every booking; the only change is where it is stored. This is the clearest example on the programme of a small process change preventing a severe service failure.

**Do not build an account system on this evidence.** F4's dissent is stronger than its support. The defensible actions are fixing email deliverability, giving customers a stable booking reference they can find, and pointing them at an existing third party tool. Revisit if a larger sample shifts the balance.

**Guide content is a genuine opportunity but an untested one.** F8 is strong on satisfaction and silent on acquisition. Any guide led content work should be framed as a test with a measurement plan, not as a finding driven certainty.

**Two recommendations are cheap enough to act on from a single source.** Tipping guidance (KA) and driver sourced restaurant lists (KA) rest on one participant each. They are included because the cost of being wrong is close to zero and both are reversible. They should not be presented with the same weight as F1 to F6.

**The strongest commercial argument in the study is not in the findings at all.** It is in F1 combined with the persona work: one organiser converts an entire party and becomes the acquisition route for the next one. CP has run group trips for twelve years and his group's standing question at the end of a tour is where they are going next. Nothing in the current product recognises, supports or formalises that. That is an inference, not a finding, and it needs testing against real repeat booking data before it is used to justify spend.

## Note added 18/09/2026: the referral inference cross-checked against the customer data

The last line of the Implications section calls this the strongest commercial argument in the study:
one organiser converts an entire party and becomes the acquisition route for the next one. It was
labelled there as an inference needing testing against real repeat booking data. That test has now
been partly run against the Spike Insight customer insights pack, and the result does not support it
as a general case.

89.3% of customers have booked once. Roughly 3% of any year's bookers return the following year.
Roughly half of apparent second bookings are the same holiday split into two transactions, and 37 to
41% of second bookings are made on the same day as the first, so the genuine return rate is lower
again than the headline. Where a second booking is a real return, it is now worth 9.8% less than the
first, and that gap has widened each year since 2023.

The two sources measure different populations, so this does not kill the argument. Spike counts
individuals in a database dominated by day tour buyers, and the referral claim is about a party
leader in a product that is a small fraction of that base. R1 organisers may behave nothing like the
base rate, and CP, who has run group trips for twelve years, is the kind of case that would not show
up in an average.

What it does mean is that the claim cannot be asserted while the base rate contradicts it, and it
cannot currently be tested properly either, because private bookings are not separable in the data
and group payment mechanics split one party across several transactions. Treat it as a hypothesis
with a named test: whether organisers of private parties rebook at a materially different rate from
the 10.7% base. Do not put it in a deck as a reason to spend.

Nothing else in this document changes. No finding, count or dissent has been edited.
