**Sample counts, rebuilt 07/09/2026.** The previously quoted figures of 18, 21 and 23 each counted something different and none survives contact with the files. 35 sessions with transcripts are held across all waves. The deck's 18 cannot be reconstructed and has been quoted externally. Full register and the arithmetic in `SOURCES.md`.

**Segments are not mutually exclusive.** At least one participant is both Segment A and Segment C: she booked a Rabbie's tour and booked other tours on the same trip with competitors. Segment counts therefore cannot be summed into a sample total, and this is not an edge case: the split-booking behaviour is exactly what the Purposeful Adventurer persona describes. Any figure presented as "N participants" must say whether it counts sessions or segment memberships.

**The boundary, stated plainly.** Non-bookers have been interviewed: Segment C covers people who chose a competitor, Segment D covers people who had never heard of Rabbie's. What is missing is Segment B, people who reached the Rabbie's site and left. Every persona is therefore still weighted toward people who chose Rabbie's, and nothing in the evidence base describes the moment of leaving.

Recruitment for Segment B has not worked. The barrier is the method: a 45 minute moderated call is a lot to ask of someone who has already discounted the brand. The planned fix is AI-moderated interviews triggered at signs of abandonment, through Flock Hub, which removes the ask-them-back problem by catching people in the moment.

This is the single research gap that would most change the confidence ratings.

## Strong. Build against these.

**The site was built for returners, not first time visitors.**
A large and growing share of traffic is first time. The proposition and USPs assume prior brand knowledge and a person in the room. Roughly 92% of visitors drop off in the interest and desire phases.

**Multi-day and day tours are structurally different products.**
Multi-day tours are trip anchors, booked before flights and accommodation. Day tours are itinerary fillers, booked after the trip exists. Different timing, different content, different conversion logic. Treating them as one funnel is the most common analytical error in this account.

**Browse intent and book intent are forced through the same front door.**
The homepage search widget demands a destination and a date before a flexible planner has decided anything. Named directly in Segment D as a need to split the inspirational research side from the actual booking side.

**Accommodation information sits in the wrong place.**
The content exists, but it is buried in a guide rather than on the multi-day tour page. One booker did not know Rabbie's booked the hotels until told in the interview. "Excludes accommodation" reads as a flat exclusion on tours where accommodation is in fact available to add. One Segment D participant mentally rejected a tour on that basis before the moderator corrected it.

**Qualification work belongs on the PDP, not in the booking flow.**
The booking flow has been doing eligibility and expectation setting work that should happen earlier. Late stage surprises drive abandonment. The checkout to purchase drop on multi-day tours is a commitment problem, not a discovery problem.

**Reviews break trust rather than build it.**
Volume is small and attribution is weak. Two Segment D participants independently lost confidence at the review section. One moved from interested to a firm no almost entirely on that basis. Photos are not tagged to specific tours.

**Filters and search dead-ends end sessions.**
Customers report filters not working. Some returning visitors gave up looking for tours they had booked before. In the day tour interview, a European tour search dead-end produced a hard stop, not a workaround: she said she would simply stop looking rather than contact Rabbie's.

**Discovery is almost entirely third party.**
Reddit, TripAdvisor, Rick Steves, Viator, GetYourGuide, personal recommendation. Cold acquisition from search alone is rare. The largest spend is aimed at the rarest way in.

**The guide is the product.**
The day tour customer attributed roughly 90% of experience quality to the guide. Consistent across personas. Driver-guides are one of only two genuine differentiators.

**Only two genuine differentiators exist.**
Driver-guides, and depth of service on logistics and accommodation. Small group size, no driving, and flexible commitment are category parity. Do not claim them as differentiators.

## Directional. Test before building.

**Booking data reflects strategy, not customer behaviour.**
Late booking patterns follow late targeting spend. Australia shows an earlier booking pattern and functions as a natural experiment. The framing: we are not found late because people decide late, we are found late because late is the only place we look.

Confidence in the mechanism is high. Confidence in the size of the recoverable early window is not. Booker interviews consistently report consideration windows of months, but as far as GA4 is concerned every one of them booked on their first visit.

**A mid-length duration gap exists in the catalogue.**
Repeatedly requested 3 to 4 day options against 7 to 8 day tours perceived as too long. Corroborated across the Purposeful Adventurer persona and two Segment D participants. Flagged as the highest long term revenue opportunity in prior research. This is a product catalogue decision, not a website one.

**Booking by proxy is a real decision path.**
A trusted friend pre-shortlists and emails links, and the recipient books from that shortlist, bypassing reviews, comparison, and most persuasion surfaces. Strong single case, conceptually consistent with group and WhatsApp decision behaviour seen elsewhere. Points at a save, share, or send-to-a-friend mechanic.

**Multi-day discoverability is weak even among satisfied day tour customers.**
A day tour booker, primed to rebook, did not know the multi-day catalogue existed. Corroborates the browser level finding with a booker voice. Post-tour cross-sell is the obvious lever.

**UK and US customers need different copy.**
UK customers already know Scotland, care more about price, book later, and trust different signals such as ABTA and ATOL. The site addresses them as first time visitors to the country.

## Single voice. Log, do not act alone.

- **Accessibility and life-stage information is missing from tour pages.** One participant spent a sustained part of her session failing to find mobility information for an 85 year old traveller, and concluded she would have to phone. Single case, but high stakes for family decisions.
- **Itineraries slightly over-promise.** A tour sold as "fishing villages" plural visited one fishing village plus an inland village, and a stop presented as included was optional on the day. Tolerated but noticed. First evidence from a completed tour.
- **Currency drives who books, not who travels.** A UK participant booked so the transaction stayed in Sterling rather than involving Australian dollars for visiting friends. Cross-border group bookings route to whoever avoids the FX.
- **"Age rating" is misread.** Two participants found "age rating: 5 years plus" confusing. Recurring across sessions.
- **Credibility badges are not self-explanatory.** B Corp confused three participants. ABTOT confused one. Matters more as AI overviews surface badges without context.
- **Referral and sign-up incentives were proposed unprompted** by one participant with a strong word-of-mouth pattern.
- **Account and app absence was raised unprompted** by one participant as the reason the post-booking relationship feels thin.

## The four personas

Adopt these. Do not reinvent them. Sequence work by confidence.

| Persona | Confidence | Market | Where the decision happens | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Meticulous Planner | 90% | US, Canada | Off site, months before first visit. Books the tour before the flight. | Strengthened |
| The Pragmatic Day-Tripper | 78% | UK, plus in-destination | One session, close to the day. | Strengthened and reframed |
| The Purposeful Adventurer | 70% | US, Canada, UK | Arrives with a list written elsewhere. | Mixed |
| The Trusted Returner | 65% | US, plus UK | Already decided. Shopping inside the site, not choosing between operators. | Indirectly supported |

**Named caveats.**

- *Meticulous Planner.* The persona under-weights the travel agent as an alternative route to the whole trip. Two Segment D participants considered one. The agent competes with the website itself, not just with other operators.
- *Purposeful Adventurer.* The product gap is confirmed. The persona's portrait, a destination-wish-list traveller interrogating the map as primary filter, is only weakly present in new visitor sessions.
- *Trusted Returner.* Not directly represented in any new visitor sample, by design. The advocacy behaviour is latent and visible in adjacencies. Treat tests as hypothesis generating.
- *Pragmatic Day-Tripper.* Reframed. Fast conversion is conditional on visible social proof, not unconditional. Thin unattributed reviews actively blocked a fast yes.

Three of the four personas make their decision before they reach the site.

## Measurement notes

- GA4 is accurate. It is also pointed at the final act of booking, which creates a targeting bias rather than a measurement error.
- The Safari seven day cookie cap and cross device identity loss compound the gap between reported and actual consideration windows.
- Booker interviews reporting 8 to 18 month consideration windows appear in GA4 as first session bookings.
- Booking data cannot validate the strategy that shaped it.
