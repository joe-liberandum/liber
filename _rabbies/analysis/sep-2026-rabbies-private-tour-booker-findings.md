# Rabbie's Private Tours: Booker Research Findings

**Prepared by:** Joe Collingwood, Sunny Lemons
**Research window:** 2 September to 16 September 2026
**Participants:** 6 private tour bookers, all converted customers
**Method:** Moderated depth interviews, 28 to 57 minutes, remote

---

## Bottom line up front

Private tour customers are not buying a more expensive tour. They are buying **someone else to take responsibility for a group**. In five of the six interviews, one person planned, paid, and carried the blame for everyone else. That person is not asking for luxury. They are asking to be told what to do by somebody who knows the country.

Rabbie's wins this business on curation and loses ground on it. Where the consultant pushed back, suggested things, and priced the answer early, the customer chose Rabbie's over a direct competitor in the same week. Where the consultant asked "what do you want to do?" and rebuilt an existing scheduled tour, the customer completed the booking but said they would look elsewhere for a more customised trip next time.

Three fixes carry most of the value and none of them are website features:

1. A booked discovery call at enquiry, run by someone with real route knowledge.
2. A structured way to capture group needs (mobility, diet, pace, ages) that does not live in an email thread.
3. Accommodation that is named, evidenced, and confirmed early enough for the customer to veto it.

The website's job in this journey is narrow but real. It has to make the enquirer confident enough to enquire, and give them a rough price before they have to ask for one.

## 1. Key insights

**1. The private tour buyer is a role, not a taste.** Five of six were the appointed organiser for a group they did not fully control: extended family, retired friends, teenagers, in laws, people with mobility needs. The purchase decision was made to remove variables from a group, not to buy a premium product. CP put the stake clearly: *"I'm the guy that is gonna take the flak from the other 15 people."*

**2. The proposal is the product demo.** For the two participants who ran a genuine competitive comparison, the proposal decided it. JT contacted Rabbie's and Discover Scotland Tours; Rabbie's gave an indicative day rate unprompted, explained why Blair Castle beat Stirling Castle for her group, and offered to buy the attraction tickets. MS contacted two companies; the other sent a stock group itinerary relabelled as private. Both chose Rabbie's on the strength of the first reply, not the website.

**3. Curation quality is inconsistent and customers can feel it.** CP and HC both described a proposal process that ran out of local knowledge. Neither is at risk of leaving; both would rebook. What they lost was the experience of being guided. CP: *"it just would seem that the private tour is just basically, we've got a public tour for 12 days. Here's the private tour for 12 days."* HC sourced her own best day of the trip, a cheese farm tour on Mull, from a blog, then asked Rabbie's to book it. KA, who had a strong experience, valued exactly the thing the others missed: *"they came back with, which I very much appreciated, too much. You're doing too much."*

**4. The customer wants to be told, not asked.** The open question "what would you like to do?" is the wrong opening move for this segment. CP: *"the questions from Rabbie's were, what do you want to do? And the answer is, I don't know. Tell me what I want to do."* KA, asked what he would change next time: *"I would go back to them and say... make me a tour."*

**5. Email only is failing at the top of the funnel and at scale.** AM finished with an email chain of 80 to 90 messages and no reliable way to find her own booking reference. HC had Rabbie's replies land in junk repeatedly, including inside an existing thread. CP and KA both wanted a call at the start and did not consistently get one. The people who were content with email, MS and HC, were the ones with the least complex trips or the most prior familiarity.

**6. Accommodation is the weakest operational link in the private product.** Three separate failure modes appeared in three separate interviews: a customer with a walker placed in a hotel with 47 steps and no lift (AM), hotels with poor recent reviews that the customer had to research and reject (CP), and a driver telling the customer that booking his own hotels had been the right call because Rabbie's sometimes books too late (KA). HC ended up in the second choice hotel in Tobermory because the first had no availability.

**7. Price is almost never the barrier; unexplained price is.** Only MS paused on price, and her concern was legibility rather than amount: *"I think there's a part of the cost where I'm not quite sure where it went to."* AM had a ceiling of around $4,000 per person and Rabbie's came in under it. KA did not price shop at all. CP treats price as a given. JT asked Rabbie's to match a cheaper quote and they did.

**8. The driver guide is the product, and the company still does not own that story before booking.** Every participant volunteered the guide as the high point, by name in four cases. KA went further and said the education was the trip: *"it was a history lesson."* None of them chose Rabbie's because of anything the website said about guides.

**9. Institutional scale is a trust asset, not a compromise.** KA rejected smaller operators out of fear: *"I had a fear of it being too small."* He had read repeatedly in a Facebook group about customers having tours cancelled on them and said *"I never felt that would happen with Rabbies."* JT made the same trade consciously against a local operator. CP values the range: *"you can pick a hostel or you can pick a castle."*

**10. The website is barely in the private tour decision, except as a price anchor.** KA looked at almost nothing. CP has known the site for ten years. MS gave it five to ten minutes. HC found the private tours tab and stopped. JT was the exception and used the scheduled tour pages to learn the country and to build a price expectation before enquiring.

**11. Rabbie's is still filed mentally as a Scotland company.** AM and MS both assumed Scotland only. AM had wanted a private tour in Europe, did not know it was possible, and gave that revenue to another operator in the same trip.

**12. There is no reliable feedback loop after a private tour.** AM and MS received no post tour survey. HC did. CP assumed everyone had. The inconsistency means the private product is being improved on the thinnest evidence of any part of the business.

## 2. Themes

### Theme 1: The organiser carries reputational risk, and buys to reduce it
**Prevalence:** 5 of 6 (JT, AM, HC, CP, KA)

The organiser is usually also the payer or part payer. They absorb the research load and the consequences. CP and KA both described the group delegating entirely to them. AM described being left to it as the family matriarch. The emotional job to be done is not "give me a great day out", it is "do not let me be the reason this goes wrong".

**Implication:** Every unresolved detail in the proposal transfers anxiety, not just admin, to the customer. The service recovery cost of a single accommodation error is far higher for this segment than for a day tripper.

### Theme 2: Group composition is the reason for the purchase
**Prevalence:** 6 of 6

The specific constraints named were: motion sickness across most of a party (JT), an 11 year old and a 70 year old in the same group (JT), a walker user and a 78 year old (AM), grandparents in their 80s who will not climb stairs and a coeliac cousin (HC), a group of 16 all aged over 60 who still want to walk (CP), and a strong dislike of crowds and strangers (MS, KA, HC's grandparents).

**Implication:** These constraints are currently collected ad hoc, in prose, inside email. That is where the AM failure originated.

### Theme 3: Curation is the differentiator, and it is delivered unevenly
**Prevalence:** 5 of 6 commented, 2 negative or mixed (CP, HC), 3 strongly positive (JT, MS, KA)

Where Rabbie's brought expertise, it won the business outright and generated advocacy. Where it did not, the booking still completed because of brand trust and prior experience, but the customer registered the gap.

**Implication:** The proposal stage is the highest leverage point in the entire private tour journey and is currently dependent on which consultant picks up the enquiry and how well they know the region.

### Theme 4: The best knowledge in the company is not in the room when the trip is designed
**Prevalence:** 2 of 6 named it directly (CP, KA), a third implied it (HC)

CP made the argument explicitly and said his driver had raised it internally too: *"realistically with the group tours, there should be more input from the people who are actually doing the tour than the people setting up the tour."* KA said the same thing from the other end, suggesting Rabbie's debrief the driver after a private trip to inform the next one.

**Implication:** There is a structural disconnect between the people who know the routes and the people who sell them. This is a process problem, not a staffing one.

### Theme 5: Communication channel preference splits by trip complexity
**Prevalence:** 6 of 6

Wanted a call or video early: AM, CP, KA, HC. Content with email: MS, HC (after the initial call). The pattern is that the longer and more complex the trip, and the less the customer already knows about Scotland, the stronger the demand for voice contact at the start.

**Implication:** A call is not needed for every enquiry. It is needed for multi day, multi person enquiries from people with no fixed itinerary.

### Theme 6: Customers do the accommodation due diligence Rabbie's should be doing
**Prevalence:** 3 of 6 (AM, CP, KA)

CP and his wife read reviews for every property and rejected some. KA booked all his own and made them fully refundable because *"stuff happens"*, citing a 97 year old father. AM trusted Rabbie's and was the one who got caught.

**Implication:** The customers who trust the process most are the ones most exposed by it.

### Theme 7: Discovery happens off site, increasingly with AI in the loop
**Prevalence:** 6 of 6 off site, 2 of 6 used AI (JT, KA)

Sources named: a Facebook group (KA, Kiwis in Haggis), Reddit threads (MS), Google (JT, MS, CP), a rival operator's personal recommendation (CP), prior Rabbie's experience (AM, HC, CP), an Instagram or Facebook advert four years prior (AM). ChatGPT and Gemini were used for shortlisting and itinerary drafting by KA; ChatGPT for comparing operator PDFs by JT.

**Implication:** Two of six used generative AI to compare operators before contacting anyone. JT could only do this because Rabbie's pages printed to PDF cleanly and other operators' did not.

### Theme 8: Value is judged against what the customer could have assembled alone
**Prevalence:** 4 of 6 (MS, KA, CP, JT)

KA ran a spreadsheet against a travel agent's quote and found a $20,000 gap with no explanation, then walked away from that agent. MS wondered where her money went given there were no inclusions. JT judged Rabbie's favourably because ticket purchasing was handled and priced honestly.

**Implication:** Every unbundled element invites the customer to price it themselves. Naming what is included, including the invisible work, protects the margin.

## 3. Segments mapped to personas

### The existing four personas do not cleanly hold this group

The current model sorts customers by **shopping behaviour**. Private tour bookers sort by **role in a group**. The result is that the same participant maps to two personas at once. CP researches like a Trusted Returner, buys like a Meticulous Planner, and behaves on site like nobody in the model at all because he barely uses the site.

### Recommended: add a fifth persona as a role overlay

**The Appointed Organiser**

*One sentence description:* The person who has been handed responsibility for a group of four to sixteen travellers with incompatible needs, who is buying delegated expertise and blame reduction rather than a premium product, and who will choose the operator that tells them what to do first.

**Primary markets:** US, Canada, with a UK resident host variant (HC)

**Defining behaviours:**
- Plans on behalf of others and is usually the payer or part payer at deposit stage
- Does not have a destination wish list and finds the open brief uncomfortable
- Screens for risk before price: operator size, cancellation likelihood, hotel reviews, vehicle condition
- Treats the first reply from the operator as the product sample
- Abandons the website immediately after enquiry; the relationship moves to email and stays there

**Decision process:** Trigger event fixes the dates (a graduation, an anniversary, a school break, a group's availability) → operator shortlist assembled from third party sources and prior experience → enquiry to two or three operators → proposal quality decides → group approval is a formality → deposit paid, often by one person for everyone

**Pain points:** Open ended "what would you like to do?" briefing; email threads as the only record; group needs captured in prose; accommodation named late or not at all; no single place to see the trip; no guidance on tipping; inconsistent post tour follow up

**Unmet needs:** A booked planning call with a route expert; a structured group needs form; named accommodation with review evidence and a veto window; an indicative price before enquiry; a post tour feedback route

**Jobs to be done:**
- "Tell me what this group should do, because I do not know"
- "Make sure nobody in my group has a bad day that is traced back to me"
- "Take the logistics off me without taking the control away"
- "Let me show the group something that looks organised before they have to commit"

**Revenue impact:** High, and concentrated. Transaction values in this sample ran from a single private day through to a 16 person, 12 day tour and a 10 day trip with a stated budget over $35,000 US. One organiser converts an entire party and becomes the referral route for the next one. CP has run group trips for twelve years and his group's standing question at the end of a tour is where they are going next.

### Mapping this sample

| Ref | Primary persona | Secondary | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| JT | Appointed Organiser | Meticulous Planner | Booked flights first, then the tour, so structurally a day tour filler but researched like a planner |
| AM | Appointed Organiser | Trusted Returner | Prior Rabbie's day tour; planned 12 months ahead; a service failure risks a high value repeat relationship |
| MS | Purposeful Adventurer | Pragmatic Day Tripper | The clearest non organiser in the sample: party of two, booked 1 to 2 months out, control and privacy driven |
| HC | Appointed Organiser | Trusted Returner | UK resident hosting US family; three Rabbie's trips; strongest advocacy in the sample |

| KA | Appointed Organiser | Meticulous Planner | Spreadsheets, refundable bookings, six months of planning, AI assisted shortlisting |

**Read across:** four of six sit on the Trusted Returner or Meticulous Planner axis already. The private tour product is being sold disproportionately to people who already trust Rabbie's or who research exhaustively. It is not yet reaching cold, low knowledge buyers at scale, and KA is the one who found it, via a Facebook group rather than anything Rabbie's owns.

## 4. Behaviour on the website

### The private tours page is an enquiry gateway, not a decision tool

Four of six spent less than ten minutes on the site before making contact. KA, asked whether he looked around: *"I would say no... because it was a private tour."* HC found the private tours tab, read it, glanced at some group tours, and enquired. MS spent five to ten minutes. CP went straight to it on the strength of ten years of familiarity.

### The scheduled tour catalogue is doing unpaid work for the private product

JT is the important case. She used the day tour pages as a learning tool and a price anchor before ever enquiring. She filtered by destination and duration, discovered the Kelpies and Aberfoyle for the first time, then ran a scheduled tour through the passenger selector purely to see what a group of her size would cost. She never reached checkout. She said upfront pricing on the scheduled tours *"helped with planning and looking at others."*

**This is the single most useful website behaviour observed in the study, and it is accidental.**

### What was looked for and not found

| Looked for | Found | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Indicative price for a private tour | No, had to email | MS scored the booking experience 9 of 10 solely because of this; JT got the day rate from the consultant's first reply and valued it highly |
| The vehicles | No, on the current page | JT remembered seeing the fleet somewhere and could not locate it live during the session; she suspected social media or an older page |
| What is included | Partially | MS wanted a more all inclusive option surfaced in the proposal process |
| Level of personalisation possible | Partially | MS said the clearer signal that private tours existed at all was what separated Rabbie's from the competitor she also contacted |
| Review dates | Not visible to her | JT wanted dates on highlighted reviews so she could judge relevance |
| Destinations beyond Scotland | No | AM and MS both assumed Scotland only |

### Enquiry form

Positively received. JT valued that it asked only enough to start a conversation: she contrasted it with forms demanding traveller ages *"when I'm just trying to get a price."* No participant reported friction in the form itself.

### Audience block ordering

JT questioned the running order on the private tours page, where passionate travellers appears above friends and family. Her reasoning was practical: *"it's hard to book a private tour just for yourself."* HC, reading the same block, identified with friends and family immediately. This sample supports moving friends and family to the top.

### Device

HC researched primarily on her phone and could not share her screen from mobile during the session, which cost us a walkthrough. Her stated reason for phone use was a slow personal laptop. The mobile private tours page has not been observed in use in this round.

### Reviews and trust signals

CP discounts onsite reviews in favour of third party sources and expects to see negatives published: *"I like sort of more third-party reviews than the actual Rabbies reviews."* JT read onsite reviews, judged them credible because they were detailed rather than uniformly five star, and wanted dates added. KA's entire operator shortlist came from a Facebook group.

### AI readability

JT printed operator pages to PDF and fed four of them to ChatGPT for a pros and cons comparison. She noted Rabbie's pages came through cleanly and some competitors' did not: *"because AI can't read like a website... Having the text."* This is a live, low cost technical advantage worth protecting.

## 5. Trends

### Trend 1: Every participant wanted the operator to lead the itinerary
**6 of 6, strongest signal in the study**

> "The questions from Rabbie's were, what do you want to do? And the answer is, I don't know. Tell me what I want to do." — CP

> "I would go back to them and say... make me a tour." — KA

> "I really appreciated the guidance... and willingness to work with me to establish an itinerary for someone who... didn't have the experience, but without making me feel... inferior." — JT

> "It makes you feel empowered, like you have a decision, but it's also like you're not paralyzed." — JT, on being given an A or B choice rather than an open question

### Trend 2: The proposal wins or loses against the competitor
**2 of 6 ran a live comparison; both switched to Rabbie's on proposal quality**

> "They weren't as upfront with information as Rabbie's." — JT, on Discover Scotland Tours

> "The itinerary and the proposal that they sent was like one of the stock proposals that I had seen online... That did not feel as much of like something that I would pay a little bit extra for, for a private tour." — MS, on the competitor she rejected

> "I really appreciate when a professional looks at me and says, don't do this, okay, do this instead, because I know more about this country than you do." — KA

### Trend 3: Local knowledge runs out at the proposal stage
**3 of 6**

> "It just would seem that the private tour is just basically, we've got a public tour for 12 days. Here's the private tour for 12 days." — CP

> "The person who was helping us didn't have the most knowledge... a lot of the activities... were things that I had researched and found versus that were suggested by the person organizing it." — HC

KA is the counter case and should not be read as a defection risk. He rebuilt his own itinerary from a failed travel agent's proposals, sent it in, and valued being corrected. His stated intent for a second trip is to hand the whole thing over:

> "I would use Rabbie's again... I'd say this is what we saw the last time, what should we do this time? I would leave it totally up to them." — KA

> "I've hit the top layer of well-known things to do. But what are those hidden gems that the locals know about?" — KA

> "I would go back to them and say... make me a tour." — KA

### Trend 4: The email thread becomes the system of record and then fails
**3 of 6**

> "I ended up with a string of emails that was probably 80 or 90 emails... it became sort of unwieldy to figure out where I was in the email string." — AM

> "It would have been far more efficient to be able to log in, have a login to a website where I could easily pull up my account." — AM

> "Sometimes the emails from Rabbie's would go to my junk... and that happened multiple times, even if it was like in the same conversation chain." — HC

### Trend 5: Voice contact is wanted at the start of complex trips
**4 of 6**

> "I like communicating on a phone, and I like communicating person to person like we are right now... versus emails." — CP

> "I'd prefer like calls, to be honest." — HC

> "If you're doing a high-end tour, I expect to get on the phone with you. We're gonna have multiple phone calls." — KA

> "Maybe their workday would be from noon to 8 some days instead of 9 to 5." — AM, on time zone coverage

AM also proposed the mechanism herself, having just used it to book this interview: *"I was able to choose a date. I was able to choose a time that worked for me within the limitations of what you had provided."*

### Trend 6: Accommodation is where trust is tested
**4 of 6**

> "I had to go up 47 steps in order to get to the room that was assigned to me." — AM

> "I think that's one thing that certainly could be improved in terms of realizing the limitations of individuals within a group." — AM

> "We had to go back to Rabbie's and say, we're not staying at that hotel. What else is there?" — CP

> "He goes, you know, I've found a couple of times with Rabbie's that if you have them make the hotel reservations, sometimes they wait too long... and you're not able to get into the type of properties that you wanted." — KA, relaying his driver

### Trend 7: The driver guide is the memory of the trip
**6 of 6**

> "It was the custom and personal experience. It was the bespoke that it was for us." — JT

> "I can't say enough about our driver who was beyond exceptional." — AM

> "Everything about that day was like because of him, and I just cannot say enough good things about him." — MS

> "Someone who's very knowledgeable, easygoing, and just like extremely proactive and helpful." — HC

> "Not only was he knowledgeable, he was just a very kind, thoughtful individual... that can make or break the whole freaking trip." — CP

> "It was a history lesson... he wove it together in a really, really meaningful and easy to relate to manner." — KA

### Trend 8: Price rarely blocks, but it must be legible early
**5 of 6**

> "From the onset, just tell me how much it costs because most times inquiries are like, is it in my budget?" — JT

> "The only reason it's not a 10 is because... I had to reach out to get the proposal and to see what the options were." — MS

> "I think there's a part of the cost where I'm not quite sure where it went to... at the end of the day, it was a tour guide with a vehicle." — MS

> "The price always shocks everybody. Everything seems to be expensive. So be it." — CP

> "I did not price shop at all." — KA

### Trend 9: Scale and permanence are bought deliberately
**3 of 6**

> "I had a fear of it being too small... the number of times on the Facebook page where customers would get called and said, my tour guide got canceled. And I never felt that would happen with Rabbie's." — KA

> "You can pick a hostel or you can pick a castle." — CP, on the range that lets economically mixed groups travel together

> "It seems like I know there's a lot to be said with like a local company, but sometimes with the organizations, it's you know what to expect with a larger company." — JT

### Trend 10: The brand is geographically boxed in
**2 of 6 explicitly, with direct revenue loss in one case**

> "I sort of had the assumption that Rabbie's was mostly a Scottish tour company." — AM, who then booked a week of Europe with another operator

> "I only presume that they did Scotland, which is my mistake." — MS

## 6. Specific topic insights

### AI in the research process
Two of six used generative AI, both in the shortlisting and comparison phase rather than for booking. KA asked ChatGPT and Gemini for reputable operators and for a ten day itinerary, then cross referenced against TripAdvisor and Google to find the consensus. JT uploaded four operator PDFs and asked for pros and cons in a table, because *"there's like a gazillion pages and some of it's filler language. Just give me the key facts."* MS and HC do not use it and prefer to research manually. The practical consequence for Rabbie's is that page content needs to survive being copied, printed to PDF, and read out of context.

### Time zones and contact hours
Every North American participant raised or accepted the constraint. Nobody regarded 24 to 48 hour email turnaround as unacceptable when planning six to twelve months out. The demand is not for faster replies, it is for a scheduled, bookable slot. AM proposed a shifted working day; KA, who has run global operations, was sympathetic but still expected phone contact at this trip value.

### The subcontracted driver conversation
KA was told his party of four was below the minimum for a Rabbie's driver and would get a subcontracted driver and a seven seat van. This was his one moment of near abandonment: *"did I choose the wrong company? Because they're not focused on what I'm doing."* He continued only because the experience up to that point had been good. The outcome was excellent, the driver was ex Rabbie's with a nearly new vehicle, but the framing of the message created the doubt. AM's St Andrews transfer was also subcontracted and she found out during the interview, not before.

### Group payment mechanics
Handled well and volunteered as a positive twice. AM's party paid via separate links per payer, structured exactly as she specified: *"it was very efficient."* CP paid the full deposit himself and Rabbie's then billed each traveller individually. KA wired funds in sterling to avoid card fees and found the instructions straightforward. No friction reported in this area.

### Accessibility and group needs
Currently captured in free text inside email, which is where it failed. AM contrasted this directly with another operator's platform that required a per person entry covering physical limitations and dietary needs: *"although it was tedious at one level, it also meant that all of that information was housed in one place."* HC managed the same problem manually, screening destinations against a coeliac cousin and grandparents who cannot climb stairs. The information exists in every one of these bookings. It is not being structured.

### Post tour feedback
Inconsistent. AM: no survey. MS: no survey, and she specifically wanted one to praise her guide. HC: received one, did not complete it. CP: assumed the group had received them. This matters twice over, because the guide is the strongest asset in the product and the feedback that would evidence it is not being collected reliably.

### Tipping
Raised once, by KA, and unresolved by the business. Because pricing is bundled he had to reverse engineer what the driver was earning from public rates and a spreadsheet. He asked Rabbie's directly and did not get a clear answer. His suggested fix is a stated range with a floor of zero for a poor experience.

### Attractions and ticketing
A quiet strength. JT valued Rabbie's buying the Blair Castle tickets and knowing the price, against a competitor who could not confirm pricing. KA noticed his driver arrived with all tickets in hand and inferred Rabbie's has access that individuals do not, having read in a Facebook group about people unable to get castle tickets. Neither knew this before enquiring.

### Dining
KA asked for structured restaurant recommendations by city, stratified the way a hotel concierge would do it, and found what he got *"kind of loose."* His group's best meal came from the driver reading them correctly over several days. HC's guide made her dinner reservations without being asked.

### Vehicle and comfort
JT's party had widespread motion sickness and screened operators partly on vehicle age and appearance, calling it superficial before making a strong case that it is not. AM found the small bus easy to get in and out of with a walker. CP praised the windows and cleanliness. KA raised the only vehicle complaint in the study: no microphone in the seven seat van, which made the guide hard to hear from the back row.

## Evidence limitations

- **Six participants, all converted customers.** This research cannot speak to why anyone enquired and did not book. The structural gap identified earlier in the programme still stands: non converting enquirers are unreachable through booker only recruitment.
- **Five of six are North American.** No participant from Australia, New Zealand, or continental Europe. UK representation is one person, and she is a UK resident hosting American family.
- **Five of six are group organisers.** The two person private tour buyer is represented by a single participant (MS). Conclusions about that subsegment are weak.
- **Recall gap.** Trips were taken between May and July 2026 and booking decisions were made between July 2025 and April 2026. Several participants checked their own emails during the session to recover detail.
- **Website pages have changed since these bookings.** JT and HC both reviewed pages live that differed from the ones they used. JT's inability to find vehicle imagery may reflect a recent change rather than a permanent absence.
- **One session lost its screen share.** HC was on mobile and could not share, so the mobile private tours page was described to her rather than observed in use.
- **Self selection.** Participants accepted an incentivised research invitation and five of six were strongly positive about their trip. Mildly dissatisfied customers are likely underrepresented.
