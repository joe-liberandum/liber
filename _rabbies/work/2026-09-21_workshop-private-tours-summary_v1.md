# Private tours research: workshop summary

**Draft, v1, 21/09/2026.** For the all-agency session, 23 September 2026, Edinburgh. Not delivered.
Source is `research/2026-Q3-pvt/findings.md` and `strategy/2026-09_private-tours-service-model.md`.
Participants are referenced by initials only.

**Where this sits in the day.** Part two, as a short evidence slot against roadmap and ownership.
Private tours is a product and commercial question rather than a journey stage, and the position
taken in the 08/09 reply to Michele was that it should not be run as a parallel agenda track. One
finding belongs in part one and is flagged at the end.

---

## The answer first

**Private tour customers are not buying a better tour. They are buying someone to take
responsibility for a group.** Five of six bookers were the appointed organiser for a party they did
not fully control. The constraints they named were group composition rather than taste: motion
sickness across most of a party, a walker user aged 78, grandparents in their 80s who will not climb
stairs, a coeliac cousin, an age range of 11 to 70, a refusal to drive on the left.

**The sale happens in the first reply, and the website is close to absent.** Four of six spent under
ten minutes on site before making contact. Where a live competitor existed, both of those bookers
switched to Rabbie's on the specificity of the proposal.

**Eight outcomes come out of this, and seven of them need no website change.** That is the
uncomfortable part for a room full of agencies. The actions land on how an enquiry is handled, who is
in the room when the trip is designed, and when accommodation gets booked. None of it can be
delivered by Sunny Lemons, Liberandum or Salience.

## The study, and its boundary

Put this on the slide, not only in the notes.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Fieldwork** | 2 to 16 September 2026 |
| **Sample** | 6 private tour bookers. Moderated depth interviews, 28 to 57 minutes, remote |
| **Composition** | 4 multi-day, 2 single day. 5 of 6 North American, the sixth a UK resident hosting Americans |
| **Boundary** | **Every participant converted.** Nothing here explains why anyone enquired and did not book |

**Three things this cannot tell us, and they matter for how the findings get used.**

Price abandonment is structurally invisible, because everyone in the sample bought. The finding is
that price did not block these six. It cannot say price does not block anyone. Using it to argue
against price transparency work would be a misuse of it.

Nothing here speaks to Australia, New Zealand or continental Europe, which have different booking
windows and different time zone realities for the call recommendation.

No mobile observation exists in this round. One participant researched primarily on her phone and
could not share her screen, so the page was described to her rather than observed.

## What changed in our thinking

The initiative asked two questions. Both are answered and neither answer is the one the tracker row
anticipated.

**"Do they want fully custom tours or private versions of existing ones?"** This turned out not to be
the decision. The product is the same route inventory either way. What separates a good private tour
from a poor one is curation, and one booker described the failure precisely: a twelve day public
tour presented back as a twelve day private tour.

**"Are they a distinct persona or a need held by the existing four?"** Neither. They are a role.
R1 The Appointed Organiser is now recorded in `personas.md`. A persona describes who someone is and
how they decide. A role describes a job someone has been handed on this trip, which any of the four
personas can be holding. In this sample the role sat on a Meticulous Planner twice and a Trusted
Returner three times, so the participants mapped to two personas at once rather than forming a fifth
cluster. There are still four personas.

**The role is probably not confined to private tours.** It was found there because that is where it
is most visible. The group and private hire segment miss, a 13-guest party that dismissed Rabbie's
because Facebook groups suggested day tours only, looks like the same role losing business on the
scheduled catalogue. That is one documented case, which is thin.

## Key findings

Ordered by evidence strength. The strength label is load bearing and should stay on the slide.

### Strong

**The purchase removes variables from a group.** 5 of 6, with consistent and specific reasoning. The
organiser carries the reputational risk personally. CP: "I'm the guy that is gonna take the flak from
the other 15 people."

**Accommodation is the weakest operational link in the multi-day private product.** 4 of 4 multi-day
bookers hit a problem, and they were four different problems. AM, a walker user, was placed in a
hotel with 47 steps and no lift, with accessible rooms available and unbooked. CP and his wife read
reviews on every proposed property and refused several. KA booked his own hotels and was told by his
driver that this had been the right call, because Rabbie's sometimes books too late to secure the
properties customers want. HC ended up in her second choice hotel. This is the only finding in the
study with no dissent in its applicable subgroup.

**Group needs are collected in prose, inside email, and that is where they fail.** 4 of 6 had
material needs and all four handled them informally. AM's walking limitation was in the email chain
and was not acted on when accommodation was booked. She named a competitor platform that required a
per traveller entry for physical and dietary limits, and said that although tedious it kept
everything in one place.

**Low knowledge buyers cannot answer an open brief and want to be told.** 4 of 6 wanted the operator
to lead. CP, asked what he wanted to do, answered that he did not know and Rabbie's should tell him.
The useful part is the dissent: one wanted a trip created, one wanted his own itinerary corrected,
one wanted neither and used a published tour as a skeleton. A single improved opening script serves
one customer in three and irritates the other two.

**The guide is the product.** 6 of 6 volunteered the driver-guide as the high point, unprompted, and
the praise was specific rather than general. No participant cited anything the website said about
guides as a reason for choosing Rabbie's.

**Voice contact is wanted once, at the start, on complex trips.** 4 of 6 wanted a call and all four
located it at the planning stage. Both dissenters were the two day tour bookers, which sharpens the
finding: demand tracks trip length and the absence of a fixed plan.

### Directional

**Where a live competitor exists, the first reply decides the sale.** 2 of 6 ran a genuine parallel
comparison and both switched to Rabbie's on proposal specificity. JT's competitor was less upfront
with information. MS's returned a stock group itinerary relabelled as private, which she said did
not feel like something she would pay extra for. The other 4 of 6 never compared at all, so the
mechanism applies to a minority of enquiries and we do not know how large that minority is.

**Rabbie's is filed mentally as a Scotland company.** 2 of 6 said so unprompted. AM gave the week
preceding Scotland to another operator because she did not think Rabbie's did Europe. Raised to
directional rather than single voice because it corroborates a pattern already established in the
earlier booker research on a larger sample.

**Institutional scale is bought as insurance against cancellation.** 3 of 6. KA had read repeatedly
in a Facebook group about guides cancelling on customers, and his party met travellers mid trip whose
operator had bailed. The thing being bought is confidence the trip will run, and size is only one way
of signalling it.

**Price rarely blocks, but unexplained price creates doubt that persists.** 5 of 6 did not treat
price as a barrier. MS is the useful case: months after the trip she still could not account for part
of the cost. Her concern was legibility, not amount.

### Process failures, small base, high consequence

**Replies land in junk and booking references get lost.** HC had Rabbie's replies land in her junk
folder repeatedly, including inside a thread she had already replied to, and chased replies sent a
week earlier. AM finished with 80 to 90 linked emails and had to search them to recover her own
reference number.

**Post-tour surveys fire inconsistently.** Two confirmed receiving one, two confirmed receiving none,
two unknown. A 2 and 2 split across four confirmed cases cannot be explained by a working process.
One of those who received nothing had a service failure worth capturing. Another actively wanted a
survey in order to praise her guide.

## Actions, in do order

Impact order and do order differ, because four of the eight add consultant or driver hours to the
pre-sale phase and are held against an unanswered margin question. Impact ranking is in
`strategy/2026-09_private-tours-service-model.md`.

### Start now. No build, no added hours.

| Action | What changes behind it | Owner | Measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fix email deliverability and issue a stable booking reference at proposal stage | Deliverability audit including replies landing in junk inside an existing thread. Reference issued by a system rather than dug out of a chain | Rabbie's, with Evolution on the technical side | DMARC aggregate reports and an inbox placement test. Proportion of consultant replies opened within 48 hours |
| Structured capture of group needs, one entry per traveller | A record the accommodation booker reads before booking. The handoff is the change, not the form | Rabbie's operations | Proportion of bookings with a complete record and a confirmed read before booking. Needs-related failures per 100 trips |
| Publish an indicative "from" day rate, with a link in from scheduled tour pages | Someone owns the rate and its caveat and reviews it. A pricing sign-off | Rabbie's commercial, with Sunny Lemons on the page treatment | Enquiry volume and proportion reaching proposal, before and after publication |
| Fix the post-tour survey trigger so every party receives one | Find out why it fires for some parties and not others. Private bookings probably sit outside the automated flow | Rabbie's CRM, with Liberandum | Proportion of parties surveyed against a target of 100%. Response rate. A guide-level satisfaction score, which would be the first the business holds |

**Why these four first.** They cost nothing, they are reversible, and two of them make everything
else measurable.

### Start now. Process change, no added hours.

| Action | What changes behind it | Owner | Measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name accommodation early, with a window to refuse it | Sourcing moves earlier. The order changes from book then tell, to propose, confirm, book | Rabbie's operations | Proportion named before deposit. Rejections inside the window, where a rising number is success. Accommodation complaints and recovery cost per trip |

**One thing to confirm in the room.** Moving sourcing earlier changes when the work happens rather
than how much of it there is. That needs confirming with whoever books the properties before it is
assumed.

### Held, pending the margin question, or run as a scoped trial

| Action | Why held | Measure if trialled |
|---|---|---|
| Triage rule separating create, correct and arrange-only, then two named options with a reason and an indicative rate in the first reply | Adds consultant time per enquiry once triage exists, though the triage itself is free | Monthly audit of 20 first replies against a rubric: options named, reason given, rate stated. Enquiry to booking rate |
| Route enquiries by region, or add a driver-guide consultation step in trip design, plus a driver debrief after each private trip | The most expensive item. Adds both consultant and driver hours | Enquiry to booking on routed against unrouted enquiries. Consultant and driver hours per private booking |
| Offer one bookable planning call on qualifying multi-day enquiries | Most requested change in the research and ranked last. Four people asking for a call is not four people who would have left without one | Take-up on offered slots. Enquiry to booking, offered against not offered. Hours consumed per booking won |

The trial is the better option on all three, because the margin comparison may not arrive for months.
Run them scoped to multi-day enquiries with hours recorded, so the cost becomes known rather than
estimated.

## What we are deliberately not recommending

Say this out loud, because each one is the obvious thing to reach for.

**No private tours page redesign.** 4 of 6 spent under ten minutes on site before making contact, and
two were walked through the current page live without finding a question it failed to answer. The
page has two jobs, a price anchor and a route in from the scheduled catalogue, and both are covered
by the indicative rate action.

**No account system or itinerary hub.** One participant wants it, three are content with email and
PDFs, and one advised Rabbie's not to turn itself into an IT company. The defensible version is the
deliverability and reference fix.

**No premium tier.** The purchase is delegation rather than luxury.

**No discounting.** 5 of 6 did not treat price as a barrier. The real price problem is legibility.

**No referral programme argument from this study.** The research implications named organiser-driven
referral as the strongest commercial argument in it. The Spike customer data does not support that as
a general case: 89.3% of customers have booked once, roughly 3% of any year's bookers return the
following year, and a genuine second booking is now worth 9.8% less than the first. The two sources
measure different populations, so this does not kill the argument. It does mean it cannot go in a
deck as a reason to spend. It is a hypothesis with a named test: whether organisers of private
parties rebook at a materially different rate from the 10.7% base.

## The two asks from the room

**The enquiry to booking rate for private tours.** It is not reported anywhere, and three of the
eight actions depend on it. Nothing in the measurement column of the service model is instrumented
today. This is the missing instrument for the whole programme and nobody has asked for it.

**The margin comparison.** There is no comparison anywhere between the margin on a private tour and
the margin on filled seats on a scheduled departure. It gates three of the eight actions, and it sits
underneath the premise that growing private bookings is worth more than filling scheduled seats. That
premise currently rests on nothing.

**A third thing that blocks sizing rather than acting.** Private bookings are not separable in the
data, and group payment mechanics split one organiser's decision across several transactions. One
party paid through separate links per payer and another organiser paid the whole deposit before
Rabbie's billed each traveller individually. A 16-person decision can present as 16 single-passenger
bookings, so average PAX per booking, which runs 1.59 to 2.13 across the four major markets, cannot
be used to size this role. The check is cheap: group transactions by shared departure, date and lead
booker.

## The one finding that belongs in part one

The decisive moment in the private tour journey is the first reply, which sits in the enquiry and
pre-tour phases. Neither phase has a single initiative on the UX backlog, and pre-tour already
carries two documented failures with nothing against them. Private tours is the third.

That is the same point the journey mapping makes from the other direction: the backlog describes the
website, and for this segment the website is not where the decision happens.
